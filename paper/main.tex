\begin{abstract}
In scan-based testing, some flip-flops remain non-scan due to timing
constraints, creating a partial-scan circuit. At $T{=}1$, non-scan flip-flop
(FF) outputs are unknown (X), causing a \textbf{31.26\,pp average gap to
full-scan coverage} across 10 benchmark circuits. We investigate how
multi-frame sequential ATPG can recover this loss. We first establish that
$T{=}2$ sequential ATPG recovers significant coverage, while $T{=}4$ adds
little further benefit, raising the central question of \emph{what mechanism
drives $T{=}2$ recovery}: the frame-based backtrack-limit differential
($T1{=}800$ at $T{=}1$ vs.\ 5000 at $T{>}1$) or Two-Phase State Justification
decoupling. Through five controlled ablation experiments on the same 10
circuits, each varying exactly one parameter, we find: (1) Two-Phase ON raises
average fault coverage (FC) from 73.48\% to \textbf{90.42\%} (+16.94\,pp),
up to +58.49\,pp on individual circuits; (2) uniform $T1{=}5000$ changes FC by
only +0.02\,pp---the backtrack-limit differential is \emph{not} the mechanism;
(3) enhanced backtrace (+0.47\,pp) and static learning (+0.00\,pp) are
negligible; (4) with Two-Phase ON, $T{=}4$ adds 0\,pp universally; (5) the
pipeline narrows the gap to full-scan from 31.26\,pp to \textbf{4.24\,pp}.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Automatic test pattern generation, partial scan, sequential ATPG, time-frame
expansion, stuck-at faults, state justification.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation}
Full-scan design grants ATPG direct controllability and observability over all
flip-flops, enabling high single-frame stuck-at fault coverage. In practice,
however, a subset of FFs may remain non-scan: converting them to scan cells
would add multiplexer delay on timing-critical paths, pushing slack negative.
Once these FFs are fixed as non-scan, the circuit becomes a \emph{partial-scan}
design, and single-frame ATPG loses coverage because non-scan FFs are not
freely controllable nor directly observable.

\textbf{Initial measurement:} At $T{=}1$ with 10\% non-scan exclusion, the
average gap to full-scan coverage across our 10 circuits is \textbf{31.26\,pp}.
This loss motivates the search for recovery through sequential ATPG.

\subsection{Problem and Discovery}
In a partial-scan circuit at $T{=}1$, non-scan FF outputs are in an unknown (X)
initial state. Any fault whose propagation path passes through a non-scan FF
pseudo-primary output (PPO) is therefore \emph{structurally untestable in a
single frame}---classified AU (atpg untestable) by the ATPG engine.

Multi-frame sequential ATPG---unrolling the circuit across multiple time frames
so that non-scan FF state can be justified through functional clocking---is the
standard approach to recover this loss. However, deeper frames increase the
search space, and standard multi-frame ATPG interleaves fault propagation and
state justification within a single backtrack search, causing propagation
backtrack explosions to exhaust the budget before state justification begins.

We implement a frame-based backtrack limit: $T1{=}800$ at $T{=}1$ (preventing
wasted search on structurally unrecoverable faults), $\mathrm{BACKTRACK}{=}5000$
at $T{>}1$ (full budget for sequential recovery). There is no per-target
timeout; the backtrack limit is the sole bounding mechanism.

\textbf{Empirical observation:} The baseline pipeline recovers 10.10\,pp (from
63.38\% to 73.48\%). $T{=}2$ and $T{=}4$ each contribute, but the recovery is
inconsistent across circuits. Some circuits gain most at $T{=}2$ (s5378:
+14.93\,pp), others at $T{=}4$ (b05: +16.69\,pp), and some show almost no
recovery (b03: +0.00\,pp, s953: +0.65\,pp). This raises the central question:
\emph{what drives recovery?}

\subsection{Approach}
We implement a \emph{progressive residual multi-frame ATPG} pipeline
($T{=}1{\rightarrow}T{=}2{\rightarrow}T{=}4$) with a frame-based backtrack
limit and fault-denominator-consistent union accounting. This pipeline serves
as the experimental platform. To discover \emph{why} recovery occurs, we design
five controlled ablation experiments (Table~\ref{tab:exp-design}), each changing
exactly one parameter, run on the same 10 circuits.

\subsection{Scope and Positioning}
This work does \textbf{not} propose a new partial-scan selection method or a new
fault model. Timing-driven non-scan FF selection uses OpenSTA
minimum-path-slack ranking at a fixed 10\% exclusion ratio---experimental setup
only. The research narrative follows a discovery path: (RQ1) What is the gap
between $T{=}1$ and full-scan? (RQ2) How much does $T{=}2$ recover, and does
$T{=}4$ add more? (RQ3) Which mechanism---backtrack-limit differential or
Two-Phase State Justification---drives recovery? (RQ4) Do enhanced backtrace or
static learning help? We report on \textbf{10 circuits} (6 ITC'99 + 4
ISCAS'89) that completed all five experiments; the remaining five circuits
(b11, b13, s9234, s15850, s35932) are excluded---b11 and s35932 produce runner
crashes, while b13, s9234, and s15850 time out under the baseline pipeline.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Full Scan vs.\ Partial Scan}
In full-scan design, every FF participates in the scan chain; ATPG can set any
FF state arbitrarily in one cycle. In partial scan, some FFs (non-scan) are
excluded from the chain. At $T{=}1$ with unknown (X) initial state, non-scan
FFs are uncontrollable---their values cannot be set by scan loading.

\subsection{Sequential ATPG and Time-Frame Expansion}
Sequential ATPG addresses partial-scan controllability by unrolling the circuit
across $T$ time frames. Each frame is a copy of the combinational portion.
Non-scan FF outputs (PPOs) at frame $t$ connect to their inputs (PPIs) at frame
$t{+}1$, while scan FF PPIs remain independently controllable at each frame.
The initial state at frame 0 is typically X. $T{=}1$ is a single-frame model
where non-scan FF PPOs remain X; $T{>}1$ provides initialization cycles to
justify non-scan FF state.

\subsection{FAN\_ATPG and PARTIAL\_SEQUENTIAL Mode}
We use FAN\_ATPG (NTU LaDS-II) extended with a \texttt{PARTIAL\_SEQUENTIAL}
unrolling mode. Non-scan FFs propagate state across frames via BUF connections;
scan FF PPIs are free at each frame. The \texttt{set\_nonscan\_ff} command
designates non-scan FFs. ATPG effort is bounded by backtrack limits rather than
wall-clock timeout.

\subsection{Fault Classes}
After ATPG, each stuck-at fault is classified as: \textbf{DT} (detected, a test
pattern was found); \textbf{AU} (atpg untestable, no pattern exists within the
search limit); \textbf{AB} (abort, backtrack limit reached before a pattern was
found); \textbf{TO} (timeout, per-target time expired); \textbf{UD}
(undetected, not targeted). Fault coverage
$\mathrm{FC} = \mathrm{DT}/(\mathrm{DT}{+}\mathrm{AU}{+}\mathrm{AB}{+}\mathrm{TO}{+}\mathrm{UD})$.

\subsection{Frame-Based Backtrack Limit}
FAN\_ATPG uses a backtrack limit to bound search effort per fault. By default
this limit is uniform ($\mathrm{BACKTRACK}{=}5000$) across all depths. Our
modification introduces a \emph{frame-based differential}: $T{=}1$ uses
$T1{=}800$ while $T{>}1$ uses 5000. At $T{=}1$, faults blocked by non-scan FF
X-state are structurally unrecoverable; without the differential the engine
spends 5000 backtracks per fault proving them untestable (AU) at significant
runtime. With $T1{=}800$, these faults quickly hit the limit, are classified AB,
and enter the residual set that $T{=}2$ recovery can attempt.

\subsection{Fault Dropping}
FAN\_ATPG performs \emph{within-run} fault dropping: after generating a pattern,
fault simulation marks other faults detected by that pattern as DT. However,
each \texttt{add\_fault --all} call rebuilds the entire fault list, and there is
no built-in mechanism to import detections from a $T{=}1$ run into a $T{=}2$
run. This motivates our custom residual fault-list loading.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}
Given a partial-scan circuit $C$ with scan FF set $S$ and non-scan FF set $N$
(selected by timing prioritization), let $F$ be the original physical fault set
extracted at $T{=}1$ (the collapsed stuck-at fault list). For time-frame depth
$T$, let $D_T$ be the set of faults in $F$ detected at depth $T$. Define:
\begin{align*}
R_1 &= F - D_1, & R_2 &= F - D_1 - D_2,\\
D_{\text{final}} &= D_1 \cup D_2 \cup D_4, & \mathrm{FC}_{\text{final}} &= |D_{\text{final}}|/|F|.
\end{align*}

A key engine-level challenge is the coupling between fault propagation and state
justification. In single-frame ATPG, D-drive and line justification operate on
the same logic cone. In multi-frame partial-sequential ATPG, fault effects
propagate within the last frame while non-scan FF state must be justified across
earlier frames---two structurally different search problems. An engine that
treats all frames uniformly applies the same backtrack budget to both, often
exhausting it on propagation before justification begins. Two-Phase State
Justification (\S\ref{sec:twophase}) addresses this decoupling.

Our analysis questions are: \textbf{RQ1} (pipeline gain): how many pp does
$\mathrm{FC}(D_1{\cup}D_2{\cup}D_4)$ exceed $\mathrm{FC}(D_1)$? \textbf{RQ2}
(stage attribution): how much comes from $T{=}2$ vs $T{=}4$? \textbf{RQ3}
(mechanism): is the gain driven by the $T1$ limit, Two-Phase, or both?
\textbf{RQ4} (enhancements): do enhanced backtrace or static learning help? For
each circuit, all comparisons use the same $T{=}1$ collapsed fault set $F$ as
denominator, since FAN\_ATPG's reported FC for $T{>}1$ uses a different
(multi-frame) denominator.

\section{Proposed Method: Progressive Residual Multi-Frame ATPG}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Progressive residual multi-frame ATPG.}
\label{tab:algo}
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Input:} circuit $C$, non-scan set $N$, fault set $F$ ($T{=}1$ collapsed)\\
\textbf{Output:} $D_{\text{final}}$, $\mathrm{FC}_{\text{final}}$\\
\midrule
1.\ \ $D_1 \leftarrow \mathrm{ATPG}(T{=}1,\ \text{target}{=}F)$\\

5.\ \ $D_4 \leftarrow \mathrm{ATPG}(T{=}4,\ \text{target}{=}R_2)$\\
6.\ \ $D_{\text{final}} \leftarrow D_1 \cup D_2 \cup D_4$;\ \
$\mathrm{FC}_{\text{final}} \leftarrow |D_{\text{final}}|/|F|$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Why Residual Targeting}
Progressive residual targeting (Table~\ref{tab:algo}) (i) preserves $T{=}1$
detections---deeper frames never re-target detected faults; (ii) reduces target
count---$T{=}2$ targets only $R_1$, $T{=}4$ only $R_2$; (iii) enables
recoverability analysis by isolating which faults are recovered at each depth.

\subsection{Two-Phase State Justification}
\label{sec:twophase}
Multi-frame ATPG on partial-scan circuits has two logically distinct
subproblems per target fault (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Phase~1 --- Propagation (last frame):} drive a D-frontier from the
fault site to an observable output in frame $T{-}1$. Non-scan FF PPIs in this
frame are treated as free PIs---the engine may assign any value to support
propagation.
\item \textbf{Phase~2 --- State Justification (frames $0..T{-}2$):} for each
non-scan FF PPI assigned in Phase~1, justify that value backward through earlier
frames. Frame-0 non-scan FF values start as X and must be justified through
functional paths.
\end{itemize}

Standard multi-frame ATPG interleaves these tasks in a single unified backtrack
search. FAN\_ATPG's \texttt{useTwoPhaseJustification\_} mode decouples them:
Phase~1 temporarily disconnects non-scan PPIs from their frame-$(T{-}2)$
drivers, treating them as PIs, and runs PODEM on the last frame with the full
budget; Phase~2 reconnects the PPIs and justifies each required value across
frames $T{-}2..0$ with a fresh budget. If any PPI value cannot be justified the
fault remains AU. This prevents propagation backtrack explosions from exhausting
the budget before justification begins. The mode is \emph{disabled in the
baseline} and tested as Exp~2.

\subsection{Fixed-Denominator Union Accounting}
To ensure fair comparison, we (1) extract per-fault $D_1,D_2,D_4$ sets using
\texttt{report\_fault} with stable (gateID, faultyLine, faultType) keys, and (2)
compute $|D_1{\cup}D_2{\cup}D_4|/|F|$ using the $T{=}1$ fault count $|F|$. This
guarantees pipeline-stage FC values are comparable within each circuit; the
headline metric is \texttt{total\_gain\_pp}.

\subsection{ATPG Optimization Flags}
Two flag-gated heuristics were implemented for ablation. \textbf{Enhanced
Backtrace:} \texttt{calCompositeScore()} computes
$0.6{\times}\mathrm{SCOAP}_{\text{ctrl}} + 0.3{\times}\text{depth} -
0.1{\times}\text{fanout}$ per candidate objective; with
\texttt{set\_enhanced\_backtrace on}, \texttt{findEasiestInput()} selects the
lowest composite score instead of SCOAP-only. \textbf{Static Learning:}
precomputes immediate fanout implications for controlling-value assignments
(AND/OR/NAND/NOR/BUF/INV); with \texttt{set\_static\_learning on},
\texttt{evaluateAndSetGateAtpgVal()} checks learned implications after each
assignment for early conflict detection.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why $T{=}2$ Recovers Faults That $T{=}1$ Misses}
At $T{=}1$, non-scan FF outputs are X; faults whose propagation requires a
specific non-scan FF value are untestable in one frame, and with $T1{=}800$ the
engine quickly aborts (AB) rather than spending 5000 backtracks proving AU. The
ablation is unambiguous: Exp~3 shows that matching the $T{=}1$ budget to $T{>}1$
changes final FC by only +0.02\,pp---non-scan FF X-state blocking, not the
limit differential, creates the residual. Exp~2 shows the recovery engine is the
decoupling: with Two-Phase OFF, $T{=}2$ recovers 7.07\,pp on average; with it
ON, 27.03\,pp---a $3.8{\times}$ improvement.

\subsection{The Role of the Backtrack Limit}
The $T1{=}800$ limit is purely a \emph{bounding mechanism}: it creates the AB
residual efficiently but does not cause recovery, as Exp~3 confirms.

\subsection{$T{=}4$ Adds Nothing with Two-Phase ON}
With Two-Phase ON, $T{=}4$ adds 0\,pp across all circuits---the post-$T{=}2$
residual is structurally AU at any depth. Without Two-Phase, $T{=}4$ adds modest
gain (up to 16.69\,pp for b05), reflecting the inefficiency of the unified
search at $T{=}2$ rather than a genuine depth requirement.

\subsection{Remaining Gap to Full-Scan}
With Two-Phase ON the average gap is 4.24\,pp. s1196 and s5378 slightly exceed
full-scan (more frames provide extra propagation paths) and s1238 is at parity;
the residual gap concentrates in b04, b05, and s953, where non-scan FF topology
yields faults with no observable path at any depth. Two sources: AU faults whose
only observable path crosses a non-scan FF output, and UD faults on the
complementary (QN) FF output, which FAN excludes from pattern generation
($\approx$0.1\,pp).

\subsection{Circuit-Size Scaling}
$T{=}2$ gain depends on the structural relationship between the non-scan FFs and
the fan-out cones of residual faults, not on circuit size. s953 ($T{=}1{\approx}37\%$)
gains +54.51\,pp with Two-Phase ON, while b08 ($T{=}1{\approx}73\%$) gains less.

\subsection{Why Enhanced Backtrace and Static Learning Fail}
Both show negligible impact (+0.47\,pp, +0.00\,pp). The bottleneck is not
backtrace quality or conflict-detection speed but the interleaving of
propagation and justification in the unified search; only the Two-Phase
decoupling overcomes it.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Sequential ATPG and time-frame expansion.} The standard approach unrolls
the circuit across $T$ cycles and applies combinational ATPG to the unrolled
model~\cite{ref1,ref2}; the FAN algorithm~\cite{ref1} underlies FAN\_ATPG.
Deeper frames increase detectability but also search cost~\cite{ref3}. We apply
this specifically to partial-scan circuits and evaluate incremental benefit via
progressive residual targeting.
\textbf{Partial scan design.} Classic methods select scan FFs by testability,
loop-breaking, or sequential-depth reduction~\cite{ref4,ref5,ref6}; recent work
considers timing and power~\cite{ref7,ref8,ref9}. We propose no new selection
method---our non-scan set is a fixed timing-slack ranking.
\textbf{Fault dropping and residual targeting.} Detected faults are dropped to
avoid redundant generation~\cite{ref10}, typically within a single run;
compaction targets multiple faults per pattern~\cite{ref11}. Our progressive
residual list stages fault dropping across sequential depth.
\textbf{Timing-constrained and low-power scan.} Scan-mux delay can exclude FFs
from scan~\cite{ref12,ref13}; test power is an active area~\cite{ref7,ref14,ref15}.
We address neither timing-closure synthesis nor test power.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
(1) Two suites only (ITC'99, ISCAS'89); no large industrial circuits. (2)
Results depend on the $T1{=}800$/5000 choice. (3) We report both B1 and B2 for
every circuit. (4) s27 is toy-scale, used for verification only. (5) $T{=}8$ not
evaluated; $T{=}4$ adds 0 gain universally. (6) Masks must be regenerated after
netlist changes. (7) FAN AU is operational (search failure within budget), not a
formal untestability proof. (8) Single ATPG backend; no cross-tool validation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We implemented a reproducible progressive residual
$T{=}1{\to}T{=}2{\to}T{=}4$ ATPG pipeline with a frame-based backtrack limit and
five controlled ablation experiments on 10 circuits at 10\% non-scan exclusion.
Key findings: (1) the baseline (Two-Phase OFF) recovers 10.10\,pp ($T{=}2$:
7.07\,pp, $T{=}4$: 3.04\,pp); (2) \textbf{Two-Phase State Justification is the
dominant mechanism}, raising FC to 90.42\% (+16.94\,pp), with $T{=}4$ adding
0\,pp universally; (3) the $T1$ limit differential does not create the residual
(+0.02\,pp in Exp~3); (4) enhanced backtrace (+0.47\,pp) and static learning
(+0.00\,pp) are negligible; (5) the gap to full-scan narrows from 31.26\,pp to
4.24\,pp. The bottleneck is not frame depth but the interleaving of propagation
and justification in the unified search, which Two-Phase decoupling resolves.
